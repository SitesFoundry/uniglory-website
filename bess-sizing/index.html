Technical guide · U.S. market

How to size a
battery storage system.

Most sizing mistakes start the same way: picking a number in kWh before deciding what the system is for. This guide works from the application down to the number, in the order the decisions actually matter.

01Start with the job

Application first

Four jobs.
Four different shapes.

The same building can need 200 kWh or 2 MWh depending on which of these it is doing.

01

Peak shaving and demand charges

The battery covers the top slice of a load profile for a short window, usually 1–4 hours, once or twice a day. High power, short duration: the energy figure is small relative to the power figure.

02

Time-of-use arbitrage

Charge in the cheap window, discharge in the expensive one. Duration is set by the length of the expensive period, not by the instantaneous load, so the energy figure grows while the power figure stays modest.

03

PV self-consumption

Store what the array produces at midday for use in the evening. Sizing is driven by the array's daily production and the evening load, and is capped by how much surplus the site actually generates.

04

Backup and resilience

Energy is set by the critical loads and how long they must survive, which is often far longer than an arbitrage window. This is the case where a modest power rating and a large energy rating appear together.

02Power and energy

Two separate decisions

kW and kWh
are not interchangeable.

A system can have plenty of energy and too little power, or the reverse. Both failures are common and both are avoidable.

05

Power, in kW, sets how fast

It determines the inverter and power-conversion equipment rating, the size of the connection, and the shortest window in which the system can deliver its stored energy.

06

Energy, in kWh, sets how long

It determines the duration at a given power. Doubling the energy at fixed power doubles the duration and changes nothing about how fast the system responds.

07

The C-rate ties the two together

C-rate is power divided by energy. A 500 kWh system delivering 250 kW runs at 0.5C, which is a two-hour system. The same 500 kWh delivering 500 kW runs at 1C, a one-hour system. The datasheet usually states the rated C-rate, and that rating is what the warranty is written against.

03Nameplate vs usable

The numbers behind the number

What the nameplate
figure hides.

Nominal capacity is a starting point. Four adjustments turn it into what the site can actually use.

08

Depth of discharge

Usable energy is nominal capacity multiplied by the depth of discharge the system is rated for. A 100 kWh pack rated at 90% DOD gives 90 kWh of usable energy, not 100.

09

Degradation over the warranty term

Capacity falls with cycling. Sizing against the beginning-of- life figure means the system misses its target in year ten. The figure to size against is the end-of-warranty retention, which the warranty document states.

10

Auxiliary loads

Thermal management consumes energy, and the share depends on the cooling design and the ambient conditions. It is small in mild climates and not small in a hot enclosure, so it belongs in the calculation rather than in a footnote.

11

Round-trip efficiency

Energy returned is lower than energy stored. For a charge-then-discharge cycle, divide the required delivered energy by the round-trip efficiency to find the energy that must be bought, and compare the cost difference against the tariff spread.

04Worked example

From requirement to capacity

Trimming a peak
by 250 kW for 3 hours.

Illustrative arithmetic, not a quotation. The figures come from the requirement, not from a catalogue.

A

Energy required at the meter

250 kW × 3 h = 750 kWh delivered to the site.

B

Adjusted for depth of discharge

750 kWh ÷ 0.90 = 833 kWh of nominal capacity, at the rated DOD.

C

Adjusted for degradation

Divide by the end-of-warranty retention the warranty states, so the target still holds in the final year of the term.

D

Pick the power rating and check the C-rate

250 kW against roughly 900 kWh nominal is about 0.28C — a gentle duty cycle. If the resulting C-rate sits above the rated figure for the product class, the energy figure has to rise or the power figure has to fall.

E

Match to the cabinet class, then parallel

Commercial cabinets in the 261 kWh class reach roughly 900 kWh with four units in parallel. Paralleling is the normal expansion path in this range, so confirm the parallel limit and the control architecture before fixing the count.

05The grid interface

Often the real constraint

The interconnection
limits before the battery does.

A battery that cannot export, or cannot charge while the site is importing at its limit, behaves differently from the one on the datasheet.

12

Export limits

Where export is capped or refused, the battery has to work behind the meter. That changes the value case and can change the power rating, because the system may never need to deliver at full rate to the grid.

13

Charge power as well as discharge power

Charging draws load. If the site is already near its interconnection limit during the cheap window, the battery cannot charge at rated power, and the achievable cycle is smaller than planned.

14

Ride-through and protection settings

Voltage and frequency ride-through, anti-islanding and protection coordination are set with the utility, not chosen from the datasheet. Confirm them before the equipment order, not after.

06Checklist

Before you ask for a price

Eight figures
that decide the answer.

A supplier can size a system from these. Without them, any capacity figure is a guess.

01

Peak demand and its shape

The interval data for a typical week, not the monthly maximum. The shape decides whether the job is power-heavy or energy-heavy.

02

Target reduction and duration

How many kW to remove, for how many hours, and how often per day. These three numbers produce the energy figure directly.

03

Tariff structure

Demand charges, time-of-use periods and export compensation decide whether the case is arbitrage, peak shaving, or both.

04

Interconnection limit and export status

The approved import and export figures, and whether any increase is possible. This bounds the whole design.

05

Site footprint and access

Available area, door widths, floor loading, and how the equipment gets to the pad. Siting constraints eliminate more options than electrical ones do.

06

Ambient conditions

Peak temperature and whether the enclosure is shaded. Thermal management capacity and derating follow from this.

07

Growth expectation

Whether the site will add load or generation within the equipment's life. It decides whether to buy the final capacity now or plan for paralleling later.

08

Timeline and phasing

Whether the battery must be live for a specific date, and whether the project can be delivered in stages.

07Sizing FAQ

Straight answers

Straight answers
on sizing.

Something not covered? Ask our team ↗

What is the difference between kW and kWh in a battery system?+

kW is the rate at which energy moves; kWh is the amount that moves. A 500 kWh system rated at 250 kW can deliver its energy in two hours. The same 500 kWh rated at 500 kW delivers it in one hour. The energy figure sets duration, and the power figure sets how fast the site can be served.

How much capacity do I need to shave a peak?+

Multiply the kW you want to remove by the hours you need to remove it for, then divide by the depth of discharge and by the end-of-warranty retention. A 250 kW reduction for three hours needs 750 kWh at the meter, which is roughly 900 kWh of nominal capacity once both adjustments are applied.

Does a larger battery always mean a longer backup?+

No. Backup duration is energy divided by the critical load. If the inverter or the interconnection limits the power, a larger battery serves the same load for longer but cannot serve a larger load at all. The critical-load list and the power rating have to be decided together.

Can storage be expanded after installation?+

Usually yes, by paralleling additional units, within the parallel limit and control architecture of the product class. Expansion is cheaper when the first installation leaves headroom in the switchgear and the site footprint, so it is worth deciding at design time even if the second phase is years away.

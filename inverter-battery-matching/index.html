Technical guide · Pairing

The pairing is
the specification.

An inverter and a battery are sold as two products and certified as one system. Three numbers on the datasheets decide whether the pair works — and a single certificate decides whether an inspector will accept it.

Check a pairing ↗ Read the FAQ ↓
01The basics

Three numbers

Voltage, current,
and rate.

Nothing else on the two datasheets changes the answer as much as these three values.

01

The DC voltage window

The inverter accepts battery voltage between a minimum and a maximum. The battery's operating range has to sit entirely inside that window — not merely overlap it.

02

Continuous current, in both directions

Charge current and discharge current are separate limits. An inverter that can push 100 A into a battery sized for 50 A charging is a mismatch even though it would discharge happily.

03

The C-rate

C-rate is current expressed relative to capacity, and it is the quickest sanity check across different battery sizes: a 0.5C rate on a 100 Ah battery is 50 A, and on a 200 Ah battery it is 100 A.

02Voltage

Inside the window

The battery range
must fit inside.

A 48 V lithium battery is not 48 V for its whole life.

01

A 48 V nominal battery spans about 40–58.4 V

A 16-cell lithium iron phosphate battery is nominal 48 V because each cell averages about 3.2 V, but it charges to 3.65 V per cell and discharges to about 2.5 V per cell. The working range is therefore roughly 40.0–58.4 V, and that is the figure to check against the inverter.

02

Check the window, not the nominal voltage

Two products labelled 48 V can still be incompatible. An inverter whose maximum DC input is 56 V cannot accept a battery that finishes its charge at 58.4 V, however closely the nominal figures agree.

03

Failures happen at the two ends

The top of the range is where over-voltage protection trips, and the bottom is where the battery management system cuts off to protect the cells. Both are everyday events, not edge cases, so the extremes matter more than the average.

03Current

Both directions

Charging and discharging
are separate limits.

The weaker of the two products sets the ceiling for the whole system.

01

The inverter's charge current must suit the battery

A battery datasheet gives a recommended and a maximum charge current. If the inverter's charge setting exceeds it, the battery ages faster or refuses the charge outright — and on many systems the setting is adjustable, so this is a commissioning decision as well as a purchasing one.

02

Discharge current is set by the load

On the discharge side the inverter draws whatever the backed-up loads demand, up to its own limit. A battery with a lower continuous discharge rating becomes the bottleneck, and the system delivers less than the inverter's nameplate suggests.

03

Derating is silent

Where limits conflict, many systems simply reduce output rather than fail. The installation works, the customer never sees an error, and the shortfall only shows up as a system that underperforms its design on paper.

04Certification

Certified together

The listing covers
the pair, not the parts.

This is the part most often missed when products are bought separately.

01

UL 9540 lists an energy storage system

The standard applies to the assembled system — the inverter and the battery together, tested as a pair. A battery that carries its own listing and an inverter that carries its own listing do not automatically form a listed system when combined.

02

UL 1741 covers the inverter's grid interface

This is the interconnection side — how the inverter behaves on the grid. It answers a different question from UL 9540 and does not substitute for it on the storage side.

03

The inspector asks for the listing

Where a permit is required, the authority having jurisdiction checks for a listed system, not for a plausible combination of parts. Ask the supplier for the UL 9540 certificate that names both products before the equipment is ordered, not after it arrives on site.

05Consequences

When they don't match

Three ways
a mismatch shows up.

Only the first is obvious. The other two cost money quietly.

01

The system shuts down

Over-voltage at the top of the charge, or a current demand above the battery's rating, triggers a protective disconnect. The inverter loses DC input and faults, and the customer experiences it as a system that stops working for no visible reason.

02

It under-delivers

Where the battery is the weaker side, the system caps output at the battery's limit. Backup duration and peak power both fall short of the design, and the shortfall is blamed on the equipment rather than on the pairing.

03

The warranty is at risk

Manufacturers write warranty terms around tested combinations. A pair that was never certified together may be excluded from cover, which turns a saving on one component into a replacement cost on both.

Straight answers

Straight answers
on pairing.

Something not covered? Ask our team ↗

Does the battery voltage have to match the inverter exactly?+

No. What matters is that the battery's full operating range falls inside the inverter's DC input window. A 48 V nominal battery typically runs from about 40 V empty to about 58.4 V full, and both ends have to be acceptable to the inverter.

What does C-rate mean in practice?+

C-rate is current divided by capacity. A 0.5C charge rate on a 100 Ah battery is 50 A, and the same rate on a 200 Ah battery is 100 A. It lets you compare a battery's limits with an inverter's charge current without converting units.

Do I need a UL 9540 listing?+

In most US jurisdictions where a permit is required, yes — the energy storage system has to be a listed assembly. Confirm the requirement with the authority having jurisdiction for the project, and ask for the certificate that names both the inverter and the battery.

Can I pair products that were never tested together?+

Electrically it may work, but it is not a listed system, the warranty cover is uncertain, and an inspector may refuse it. For anything that needs a permit, the tested pairing is the safer and usually the cheaper route.
